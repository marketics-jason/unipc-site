The emblem

An open council, gathered over a shared horizon.

IS

The mark is built entirely from structure, not from symbol. Every element — the circle, the assembly, the horizon — is a universal idea that no single people owns. It could be adopted by any nation without asking another’s permission. That was the first rule of its design, and the last test it had to pass.

What each element means.

Read from the outside in: the council that holds the room, the peoples who gather, the ground they share.

The open ring

A council in session — the circle every nation can sit around as an equal. It has no head of the table, and no beginning or end.

The assembly

Seven points rising in an arc — nations taking their place. It reads as a constellation, as an assembly, as many becoming one line without any losing its position.

The horizon

One line the whole assembly shares — the common ground, and the open future beyond it. The single jade point is where a nation meets the world.

The orbits

Concentric rings hold the assembly without enclosing it — protection, not a fence. Confluence: many circles drawn to one centre.

Principle · what the mark refuses to be

If an element would need any one nation’s permission to use, it is not in the mark.

×No koru, wampum, wiphala, or dot technique — no borrowed sacred pattern.
×No dreamcatchers, feathers, or arrowheads.
×No “tribal” borders or pan-Indigenous clip-art.
×Nothing that pictures one people to stand for all.

Treated as a seal, never as decoration.

The emblem carries the authority of the institution. It is placed with restraint, given room to breathe, and never animated for ornament.

Clear space

Keep a margin of at least the ring’s radius on every side. Nothing crowds the seal.

Minimum size

Below ~24px the assembly simplifies to three points and a horizon — never let it blur.

Two registers

Clay on light for the day register; light with an amber assembly on slate for the dark. No other colorways.
